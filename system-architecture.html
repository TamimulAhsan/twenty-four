TwentyFour — Platform Architecture

One operating system for small and medium businesses — website, bookings, point of sale, payments, marketing and customer records. Sold as modules, provisioned per tenant by a specialist, live within 24 hours. Every box in this document is an independently deployable microservice; all of them run once and are shared by every tenant.

Client / edge / frontend Microservice Control plane Data store / bus External / forked OSS Synchronous call Asynchronous event
Two decisions everything else follows from

Modules, not microservices, are the unit of sale. "POS" is a commercial bundle that switches on several services and pulls in its dependencies automatically. The Module Registry owns that mapping.

Provisioning is data, not deployment. No pods are created per merchant. Provisioning writes an entitlement record, seeds tenant data from an industry template, and connects external accounts. The gateway enforces entitlement on every request.

Overview · 01

Request routing across three planes

The platform serves three audiences that must never share an authorisation path. The merchant's own customers browse a tenant storefront. Merchant staff run their business from the merchant dashboard. Your specialists administer every tenant from the admin console.

Host NGINX does nothing but terminate TLS, map merchant custom domains and proxy into the cluster — no serving happens there. Inside, Traefik splits host-based routes to the NGINX frontend pod, which serves the static SPA bundles, from API traffic to the two gateways. The stack and the reasoning behind each proxy are in §17.

Why two gateways

The admin plane can read and mutate every tenant, so it does not share an ingress path, an auth realm or a session cookie with tenant traffic. Staff sign in through SSO with MFA behind an IP allowlist, and every admin action is written to the audit log before it reaches a service. A single gateway with a role check would make one RBAC bug a cross-tenant breach.

Overview · 02

Deployment topology — one environment per market

The platform is deployed once per market, on its own VPS. Hungary runs on Hungarian infrastructure; Bangladesh runs on Bangladeshi infrastructure. They share a codebase and share nothing at runtime — no database, no event bus, no traffic, no tenant.

Every environment runs the identical stack except for three pods — Payments, Invoice & Receipt and Subscription Billing — which are replaced with that market's implementations. Because an environment serves exactly one market, no service anywhere needs to know which country it is running in. That is the point: market variation is handled by deployment, never by code.

What this buys, and what it costs

Consequence
No country logicNo branching, no adapters, no per-tenant country resolution. The single largest source of accidental complexity in multi-market products simply never enters the codebase.
Data residencyHungarian data stays on Hungarian infrastructure, Bangladeshi data on Bangladeshi. GDPR and local data rules are satisfied by topology rather than by policy.
One ledger per marketOne environment, one legal entity, one currency, one set of books. No multi-entity ledger, no consolidation logic inside the product.
Blast radiusA bad finance release in one market cannot touch another. A provider outage is contained to one country.
Cost: infrastructure multipliesEach market is a full stack — roughly 35 workloads and 75–85 pods at production replicas. Two markets is two of everything.
Cost: releases fan outA change to shared code has to be rolled out to every environment. Versions will drift unless deployment is automated from day one.
Cost: no group-level viewNothing sees across markets. Consolidated MRR and group financials need a separate reporting step that pulls from each environment.
Decided: one admin console per market

Each environment has its own console, seeing only its own tenants. A specialist working across markets signs into each separately. This matches the isolation exactly and keeps the console a normal part of the stack rather than something that has to reach across boundaries.

The consequence to plan for is group consolidation. With one ledger per entity, nothing adds up the group. Early on that is a spreadsheet pulled from each environment's reporting API; if it matters later it becomes a small warehouse each environment exports into. Worth naming an owner before the first board pack rather than after.

Overview · 04

Module catalog

What the product is actually made of, from the merchant's point of view. A module is the unit a specialist provisions and the unit the gateway enforces. It is not the unit of price — a tenant sits on a subscription tier, and the tier decides which modules they get.

Modules do not map one-to-one onto services. Selecting POS switches on one service and pulls in three more, because an order needs something to price it, something to decrement, and something to charge. Those dependencies resolve automatically in the admin console; the specialist sees what got pulled in.

Core modules are deliberately trade-neutral. Anything specific to one trade is a sold module, a trade capability switched on by the industry profile, or just a profile setting — see the note below the dependency graph.

Every core module must be trade-neutral

The platform ships 40+ industry configurations, so a module that assumes one trade is a module that is wrong for most tenants. POS means "register a sale" — nothing more. A candy shop, a boutique and a salon all need it, and none of them has a kitchen.

Trade-specific capability lands in one of three places. The test is whether a merchant would ever choose it, and whether it has a surface of its own:

  • Sold module — a merchant chooses it and it belongs to a tier. CRM, Marketing & Ads, AI Creative.
  • Trade capability — it has its own screens, devices and workflow, but nobody chooses it: the trade decides. Kitchen Display is the worked example. It ships inside POS & Orders and is switched on for food-service tenants only. A candy shop buying POS gets a till; a restaurant buying the same POS gets a till and prep screens. Table and floor-plan management would follow the same rule.
  • Industry profile setting — only terminology, fields, rates or layout. Whether the catalog is called a menu, a price list or a treatment list; which tax categories exist; what a receipt looks like. No new service, no new code path.

The principle survives all three: POS still means "register a sale" everywhere. The candy shop never sees a kitchen display because their profile does not enable it, not because POS behaves differently. If a description of a sold module names a trade, that is still the bug.

Four tiers. The tier is what a merchant buys and what they are billed for; the module set follows from it. Enterprise is the exception — it is a custom-provisioned tier whose module set is agreed per customer, which is why it has no fixed contents and no list price.

Seat counts are provisional and expected to move. Prices are not set. Neither is an architectural input — both are values in the Registry, changed without touching code.

Two perks ship after launch

Custom domain (Max) needs per-tenant TLS issuance and renewal at the Host NGINX layer. API access (Enterprise) needs a gateway surface plus a small service issuing keys, scopes and per-tenant rate limits. Neither exists today.

Both are marked coming soon on the tier page rather than being quietly implied, so a merchant on Max knows a custom domain is queued rather than missing. The Registry carries that state per perk, which means the label disappears when the work lands — no release needed to stop advertising something as forthcoming.

Staff seats are a quota, not a module

The seat count is a limit held on the entitlement record, sitting alongside the module set rather than inside it. It is unrelated to the Staff & Rota module, which is about shifts and availability and arrives as a Bookings dependency in every tier.

One seat means one person across both surfaces — the merchant dashboard and that tenant's Twenty workspace. So the limit is enforced in two places from one number: the Staff service refuses the seat, and CRM Sync must refuse to provision the corresponding workspace member. Enforcing it only in the dashboard would let the CRM drift past the tier silently.

Tiers grant modules; modules never carry a price

The Module Registry holds three things: the dependency graph above, the module set each tier grants, and whether each module can be provisioned without a human. A specialist or a merchant picks a tier, the module set follows, and dependencies are applied on top.

Where a tenant needs something outside their tier, that is an entitlement override recorded against them — visible in the admin console and the audit log, and deliberately not something that silently moves the price. Enterprise is simply the case where the override is the tier.

Because an environment serves a single market, the picker offers exactly what that deployment supports. There is no country filter applied at runtime.

The industry profile

One input decides everything trade-specific: business type. A specialist records it during the intake call; a merchant signing up themselves picks it from a selector on the onboarding form. It resolves to an industry profile, and the profile carries four things:

  • Trade capabilities — which are switched on, such as Kitchen Display for food service.
  • Term set — what to call things, covered below.
  • Catalog template — the starting menu, room types, treatment list or product set seeded at provisioning.
  • Documents and tax categories — receipt layout, invoice wording, which tax classes exist for this trade.

So what a tenant is entitled to is the union of three sources, and it is worth being explicit because they are set at different moments by different people:

What lands on the entitlement record

entitlement = tier module set ∪ industry profile capabilities ∪ manual overrides

The tier is chosen and paid for. The profile follows from the business type and costs nothing. Overrides are recorded per tenant by a specialist. The gateway checks the resulting set and neither knows nor cares which source put an entry there — which is what keeps a trade capability from needing its own enforcement path.

Trade vocabulary

A hotel calls a catalog item a Room; a spa calls it a Service; a restaurant a Dish; a shop a Product. Same concept, same tables, same API — different word on the screen. With 40+ trades this cannot be handled by branching, and it must not be confused with language translation.

Keep the two axes separate. Language is ordinary i18n. Trade vocabulary is a term set: a mapping from a stable semantic key to a word, layered on top of the language bundle. The code only ever knows the key.

How it is delivered

  • Term sets are content, not a service. They ship versioned with the industry templates. Tenant Profile stores only which trade a tenant is and any overrides, so no new deployment and no network hop to render a word.
  • The dashboard fetches them once, at login — alongside entitlements, in the same bootstrap call, cached in Redis the same way and invalidated by the same kind of event. One round trip returns what a tenant may see and what to call it.
  • It is an overlay on the language bundle, not a parallel system. Load hu-HU, overlay the hotel term set, and t() keeps working unchanged. Trades multiply by languages as content, never as code paths.
  • Services that render text need it too. Invoices say "Room", not "Item". Booking confirmations say "reservation", not "appointment". Invoicing, Notification, the storefront and the Twenty object blueprint all resolve from the same set.
  • The admin console needs a term set editor. That is what makes trade 41 a specialist filling in a dozen fields rather than an engineering ticket.
The rule that makes this safe

A display term is never a key. Database columns, API paths, event payloads, analytics dimensions and search fields all stay semantic — catalog_item, never room. If /api/rooms ever exists, the abstraction has already failed and the hotel vocabulary is welded into the platform.

Terms are presentation only. Renaming one is a content edit that takes effect on next load — it must never imply a migration, and it must never change what a report counts.

Why entitlement is checked at the gateway

The check has to happen once, in front of everything, or it will eventually be forgotten in one service — and that one service is a cross-tenant data leak. The gateway resolves tenant and module entitlement from Redis on every request, invalidated by module.enabled and module.disabled events rather than by a TTL, so a change takes effect immediately instead of whenever a timer expires. Domain services stay ignorant of who bought what; they only ever see an already-authorised, already-scoped request.

The merchant dashboard builds its navigation from the same record via GET /me/entitlements, so the UI and the API can never disagree about what a tenant has.

The contract is the whole design

Swapping a pod only works if nothing upstream can tell the difference. The value of this model lives entirely in the interface those three services expose — it has to be stable, market-neutral, and free of any assumption that happens to be true in the first market you build.

Concretely: no field named for a card, no assumption that a payment settles instantly, no assumption that an invoice number is issued locally, and no currency-specific rounding baked into a caller. POS asks for a payment and gets a result; it never learns that Bangladesh answered with a mobile wallet and Hungary with a card.

Mechanisms · 10

Modules, entitlement and provisioning

The mechanism that makes the platform modular. A specialist picks modules in the admin console; the Module Registry resolves their dependencies; the Provisioning Orchestrator writes entitlements and seeds tenant data. From then on the gateway rejects any call to a module the tenant does not hold.

A tenant is born one of two ways: a specialist creates it after an intake call, or a merchant signs up themselves and lands in a pending state for a specialist to pick up.

Changing tier later works both ways too. A merchant can upgrade themselves from the subscription page in their dashboard, and a specialist can do it for them from the admin console. Both routes converge on the same Registry resolution and the same provisioning saga — there is no separate self-serve code path to keep in step.

Modules govern access; tiers govern price. There is no per-module pricing. A tenant sits on a subscription tier, and the tier defines the module set they get. Entitlement is still evaluated per module — that is what the gateway checks and what the dashboard renders from — but the module count never enters a price calculation. Changing tier is what changes the bill.

Cross-cutting concerns

Tenant isolation

Every service owns a private schema in PostgreSQL — no service reads another's tables; it calls the owning service or consumes its events. Within those schemas, isolation is by mandatory tenant_id with row-level security. That is the right default at SMB volume and lets one shared deployment serve every merchant. A per-tenant schema or database is worth keeping as an enterprise-tier option, but designing for it now would cost you the economics that make the product work at this price point.

Security boundaries

  • Admin plane separation. Its own gateway, auth realm, MFA and IP allowlist. No shared session with tenant traffic.
  • Impersonation is scoped and expiring. A support token names the tenant and the permissions it grants, expires on a timer, and emits impersonation.started/ended to the audit log. It is never a long-lived superuser session.
  • Entitlement is a security control, not a UI concern. Hiding a nav item is not access control; the gateway rejection is.
  • The audit log is append-only and consumes from Kafka rather than being written to directly, so a compromised service cannot rewrite its own history.

Money, currency and jurisdiction

  • Never store money as a float. Integer minor units plus an explicit ISO currency code, everywhere, including in event payloads.
  • Rounding belongs to the market's own implementation, not to a language default. HUF and BDT do not round alike, and getting it wrong shows up as minor-unit drift that reconciliation only surfaces months later.
  • An invoice is immutable once cleared. Corrections are new documents that reference the original — never edits. Both NAV-style real-time regimes and NBR expect this.
  • Historic documents re-render exactly as issued. A rate change must never retroactively alter last year's invoices, so the market's Invoice service stores the rendered artifact rather than recomputing it.
  • A government API being down must never block a sale. Issue, queue, submit, reconcile. The market's Invoice service owns that queue and its alerting.

Failure and retry semantics

  • Events are at-least-once. Every consumer must be idempotent — keyed on the event ID, or on a natural key like the order reference.
  • Provisioning steps are individually retryable and carry their own state, so a partial failure is resumable rather than restart-from-scratch.
  • External calls are the failure surface. Card processor, ad platforms, comms providers and the LLM API all need timeouts, circuit breakers and a dead-letter path. A slow processor must not take checkout down.
  • Cache invalidation is event-driven, not TTL-driven, for entitlement. A module switched off must stop working now, not in five minutes.

Why ClickHouse is fed only from Kafka

Services double-writing to an analytics store drift from the system of record the first time one write succeeds and the other does not. A single CDC and event sink connector means ClickHouse is a projection of what actually happened, and the reporting API is the only thing that reads it.

Why Traefik there and NGINX everywhere else

NGINX is faster, leaner and better at serving static files — which is exactly why it keeps the two jobs where those properties matter: terminating TLS at the host edge, and serving the SPA bundles.

The in-cluster ingress needs something different: it watches the Kubernetes API and reconfigures itself as services change. NGINX's static-config model is a liability there, and ingress-nginx — which worked by regenerating nginx.conf and reloading — has been retired by the Kubernetes project. Do not reach for it. Traefik is MIT-licensed, ships with k3s so there is nothing to install, and speaks both the Ingress API and its successor the Gateway API.

Four things the table does not say

  • The transactional outbox is a pattern, not a product. Nothing above gives you atomic "write to Postgres and publish to Kafka". Without an outbox table drained by a relay you will lose events or emit phantom ones — and inventory, the ledger and the CRM all derive from events. This is the highest-value thing to get right first.
  • Contract tests are what make the market-swappable design real. Payments, Invoicing and Billing are only interchangeable if a new market's implementation must pass a suite before it deploys. Without that, "swappable" is an aspiration rather than a property.
  • River now, Temporal later. Temporal is MIT and genuinely better at durable workflows, but it is several services plus its own datastore, multiplied by every market VPS. River uses the PostgreSQL already running. Model the provisioning saga as an explicit state machine and move to Temporal when a second long-running workflow earns it.
  • Twenty is not Go. The CRM fork is NestJS and TypeScript, so the stack carries a Node runtime, a second build pipeline and a second dependency-scanning surface. Not optional — it follows from owning the fork.

Deliberately not added

  • Keycloak. With Twenty authenticating by shared session cookie rather than OIDC, the main reason to run a separate identity provider disappears. A Go Auth service with argon2id and PASETO, plus TOTP for the admin plane, is less code and far less weight than a Java IdP.
  • A search engine. PostgreSQL full-text search until a specific query is demonstrably too slow. Meilisearch or Typesense then, not before.
  • Redpanda. Attractive operationally — single Go binary, no JVM — but it ships under the Business Source Licence, which is source-available rather than open source.
  • Our own SMTP. Deliverability at volume is IP warmup, reputation management and blocklist recovery. A full-time job unrelated to the product.
The boundary open source does not cross

Delivery and payment rails terminate at commercial providers: SMTP, SMS, card and wallet processing, ad platforms and the LLM API. Self-hosting is not the goal there, and in several cases is not possible.

Note where they sit architecturally — inside the market-swappable boundary. Hungary and Bangladesh will not share an SMS aggregator any more than they share a payment provider. Provider credentials and adapters belong in per-environment configuration; the Notification service itself stays market-neutral.
